## A tight daily ritual loop.
One puzzle, once a day, at a fixed reset time. Scarcity creates anticipation — you can't binge it, so you come back tomorrow. The daily cadence also anchors the habit to an existing routine (morning coffee, commute, lunch break).

## Fast time-to-first-satisfaction.
The first few moves always feel productive. In Wordle you get immediate feedback on every guess; in Connections you can often spot one group quickly. This hooks you before any frustration sets in.

## Calibrated difficulty with a fixed endpoint.
There's always a way out — you only need 6 guesses, or 4 groups, or however many letters. The ceiling on effort is visible. Players aren't grinding; they're solving a bounded problem, which feels achievable even when it's hard.

## Emotional variance within a simple ruleset.
The rules are learned in under a minute, but the emotional range is huge — smugness when you get it in two, despair on the purple group, the specific frustration of a one-away failure. That emotional texture is what people actually share.

## Social shareability without spoilers.
The emoji grid format (🟨🟩⬛) was a masterstroke — it lets you broadcast your performance and invite comparison without ruining anyone else's solve. This turns a solo experience into ambient social currency.

## A sense of authorship and personality.
The NYT Crossword has constructors with names; Wordle answers are curated (controversies about "AGORA" or "TACIT" become news). Connections has clear thematic wit. Players feel like there's a human sensibility behind the puzzle, not an algorithm — which creates something to react to and talk about.

## Streak mechanics and implicit identity.
Wordle's streak counter is devastatingly effective. Losing it feels genuinely bad, which means protecting it becomes a small daily mission. Over time people start identifying as "I've never missed a Wordle" — the game becomes part of their self-image.

## Appropriate friction on sharing.
You have to finish (or at least play) to share. The share button is the reward for completing, not an escape hatch. This distinguishes it from passive content consumption.

## Cross-demographic accessibility.
The vocabulary and cultural references need to be broad enough that a 24-year-old and a 65-year-old can both feel the game is for them. NYT Connections occasionally fails this (very Gen-Z or very boomer-coded groups), and players notice.
